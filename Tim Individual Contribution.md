### Timothy Donkin: Individual Contribution

For my individual part, I wrote the Ethics, Privacy and Security section of the report. This section covered the ethical considerations around the distance-threshold artefact in the FARA classification (rural states ranking higher regardless of actual deprivation), the risk of the race/ethnicity findings in Figure 6 being read as causal rather than structural and the limited generalisation of a US dataset to a New Zealand context. This section also covered the privacy implications of cross-tabulating variables across our merged sources, as well as outlining our security measures, both what we implemented and what we would do next time. 

For the EDA I built the scatter plot of poverty rate against low food access in Python, this became Figure 3. This plot was important because it tests a common assumption directly, that poverty is a primary driver of food access outcomes, and the result actually showed us that there is a weak relationship across the range of poverty rates. This motivated our later investigations in further figures. I also built the initial version of the grouped box plot comparing low food access by race/ethnicity bins and income tier, which Kaiyuan then refined into Figure 6. 

I also built Figure 5, a choropleth map of low food access by state, built on vectorised data from our sources. This replaced the group’s bar chart of the top ten states in this category. I aggregated the tract level data to the state level using the same classification threshold as the rest of the report (33% of tract population or 500 people meeting the low access distance criteria). I sourced the US State boundary geometry through the R-ArcGIS bridge and classified states into 5 groups using Fisher’s natural breaks. The full state map was decided on the basis that the original bar chart did not provide enough information. The state map was optimal because it allows for a broader analysis rather than confining to the top 10, as well as allowing us to have an overview of the US as a whole split into these classifications, showing any geographic patterns that may appear. Originally the classification was done with Jenks, but after receiving errors I switched to Fisher’s natural breaks because it uses a closely related algorithm that did not produce errors.
